A small group of traders with faster cables and closer servers could see your order coming, and trade ahead of it on other exchanges. A bank trader, Brad Katsuyama, proved it, built a router to beat it, then built an exchange to remove the advantage.

Problem

Vanishing liquidity

Katsuyama's screen showed shares offered. When he clicked buy, the offers disappeared before his order arrived, and the price moved up.

Cause

Fragmentation + speed

One order was split over many exchanges. Orders reached them at different times. Fast traders saw the first arrival and pulled quotes on the rest.

Fix

Make arrival simultaneous

Thor delayed the nearer legs so every piece landed at once. IEX then built the delay into the venue itself.

Timeline

  1. 2001

    Decimalization. Minimum tick falls from 1/16 to one cent. Spreads shrink, so market making becomes a volume-and-speed business.

  2. 2005–07

    Reg NMS. Order protection and a national best bid and offer (NBBO). Exchanges must be reachable fast, so competing electronic venues multiply.

  3. Late 2000s

    The puzzle. At RBC, Katsuyama sees quotes vanish as he trades. Ronan Ryan and the team trace it to network timing.

  4. 2009

    Sergey Aleynikov. Goldman programmer arrested for copying code when he left. Lewis uses the case to show how valuable the trading code is.

  5. 2010

    Spread Networks line. Near-straight fiber Chicago to New Jersey, about $300M. Round trip about 17 ms down to about 13 ms.

  6. 2010–11

    Thor at RBC. Router that staggers order departure so all legs arrive together. Fills on displayed size improve sharply.

  7. 2012

    IEX founded. Katsuyama leaves RBC with Ronan Ryan and John Schwall.

  8. 2013

    IEX dark pool launches with a 350 µs speed bump.

  9. Mar 2014

    Flash Boys published. Debate over whether the market is "rigged" goes mainstream.

  10. 2016

    SEC approves IEX as a national exchange. The speed bump is allowed as a protected quote.

Contested

What critics say

Lewis writes as a journalist with a hero. Academic work finds HFT narrowed spreads for small orders, while also finding the latency race is a real cost (Budish, Cramton and Shim, 2015).

"Front-running" here is not the legal kind. It is speed-based information advantage. Say that precisely in an interview.

Six things to remember: (1) fragmentation creates arbitrage, (2) speed decides who sees news first, (3) stale quotes get picked off, (4) rebates bend routing, (5) simultaneous arrival kills the trick, (6) a tiny bump protects resting orders.

Summary of the book and public sources. Figures are approximate and animations are illustrative, not to scale. Check any number before quoting it in a live interview.

Say it like this: "The order reached the nearest venue first. A co-located trader saw that, and cancelled or repriced its quotes on the farther venues before my slower legs arrived. Thor removes the timing gap by delaying the near legs."
Maths: glass carries light at about two thirds of c, roughly 200 km per ms. Fiber path 827 miles ≈ 1,330 km, so ≈ 6.7 ms one way, ≈ 13 ms round trip. That matches the number in the book. Air is close to c and the path is straighter, so microwave wins.
Say it like this: "A market maker earns the spread plus any rebate, and loses when informed flow hits a stale quote. Queue position sets both the fill odds and the adverse selection you take."
Say it like this: "Maker-taker pays whoever posts liquidity and charges whoever takes it, capped at $0.003 a share under Reg NMS. Brokers that route to the highest rebate can worsen the client's fill."
Say it like this: "The bump delays all inbound orders equally, but IEX's own market data is not delayed. The venue reprices before a stale-quote snipe arrives, so resting orders are protected."

The book gives you the story. These are the questions interviewers turn it into. Open each and answer out loud first.

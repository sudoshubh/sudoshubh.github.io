LECTURE 1

What computer vision actually is

perception · recognition levels · challenges · efficiency · Marr's framework · SIFT · HOG

The definition she wanted

Computing properties of the 3D world from visual data — algorithms and representations that let machines recognise objects, the relationships between objects, people, scenes and activities. The word she kept returning to is perception, and her point about it is that perception is trained by life experience. Two people looking at the same market scene describe it differently. That subjectivity is exactly what we are trying to transfer into an autonomous agent.

What “recognition” actually involves

Object categorisation

Not one object type — multiple. People, trees, mountains, buildings, street lamps, vendors.

Instance recognition

This is not just any street; it is that particular location.

Scene & context

Outdoor, within a city, crowded. Context changes the interpretation.

Attribute recognition

Grey, made of fabric, flat, crowded.

Why a 2D image is not enough — Marr's framework

A 2D image is ambiguous and incomplete. The same object looks completely different under changes in lighting, distance, viewpoint, background and occlusion. So the human system, and the classical computational model after it, works in stages:

Input imageRaw intensities. Pixels tell us what is visible.
Primal sketchEdges and boundaries. Edges tell us where structure begins and ends.
2½-D sketchDepth and surface orientation. How structures are arranged relative to the viewer.
3D modelVolumetric primitives, hierarchically organised. Now we know what the object is.
Exam favourite — why 2½-D and not 3D? Because the depth information is relative to the observer's viewpoint only. You have added information beyond X and Y, but you have not reached a full volume model: you still know nothing about the object from any other viewpoint. It is halfway, hence the half. She pushed the class hard on this one and rejected “it's still x and y” until viewpoint was named.

On occlusion, her actual answer: if an object is completely hidden from a single viewpoint, a human could not find it either, so do not expect the model to. The research answer is collaborative multi-camera edge computing — several cameras share information and process jointly. From one camera and one viewpoint, it is very difficult.
The efficiency argument, in her numbers: the brain processes one image in roughly 20 KB; an AI model doing the same work takes on the order of 20,000 KB. That gap is why our algorithms are still not efficient, and efficiency is the precondition for real time. Three costs to name: time, memory, energy. Scaling means processing more frames within a fixed time budget.

Where limited data is guaranteed

She asked the class to name domains where you know in advance you will have few samples. Answers she accepted: medical records, rare diseases, rare cancer subtypes, specific autism subtypes, volcano imagery, geolocation tagging. Her addition: in medical cases the usual data-multiplication techniques often do not work, so the scarcity is real rather than fixable.

Features, SIFT and HOG

Her definition of a feature, arrived at after rejecting several: distinct information — whatever helps you distinguish one thing from another. Redundant information is not a feature.

A good feature point is…

  • Distinctive — carries information you cannot recover if you lose it
  • Stable — not flickering in and out under processing
  • Repeatable under scale, rotation, lighting and viewpoint change

SIFT

Classical feature detection, designed to be invariant to scale and rotation. Rotate or scale a building and the same key points are still found — which is what lets you undo a distortion afterwards.

Gradient

She would not accept “slope.” The definition is rate of change. It carries two pieces of information: magnitude (how important) and direction (alignment).

HOG — histogram of oriented gradients — builds a descriptor out of exactly those two quantities. It describes appearance and structure.

The question she liked best: do we still need handcrafted descriptors now that we have CNNs and transformers? Her answer: yes. On genuinely constrained hardware — thermal cameras, devices with kilobytes of memory — a CNN cannot be deployed at all, and HOG is more efficient than any YOLO variant. Less accurate, but it works. Secondly, such filters can be used inside the early layers of a CNN, because CNNs are hierarchical and early layers are doing edge-like work anyway. She explicitly rejected “because CNNs overfit” as a reason — overfitting is a design failure by the human, not a property of CNNs.

Where HOG fails: very low contrast, irregular or highly curved shapes, random patterns with no consistent gradient direction, occlusion, and dense environments.

LECTURE 2

The task landscape, CLIP, and how images are stored

3D reconstruction · ImageNet · architecture turning points · CLIP · colour models · compression · I/P/B frames

3D reconstruction from unconstrained photos

Her ICCV 2009 example: build a coherent 3D model from many 2D photographs taken by different people, on different hardware, at different times, under different lighting. The two things that made it work were camera position and viewing angle — find matching points across images, estimate where each camera was standing, then merge.

ImageNet and why architectures got bigger

ImageNet was started in 2004 by Fei-Fei Li — she called her the godmother of AI — and grew to roughly 22,000 categories and over a million images. The 2009 large-scale challenge used 1,000 object classes. It was hard for a specific reason worth stating in an answer: it stresses both inter-class and intra-class variation at the same time.

The causal chain she wanted: big dataset first → then the bottleneck moves to model capacity. A small model on a large dataset underfits — the model simply does not have the capability. That is what pushed the field into architecture research: LeNet → AlexNet → GoogLeNet → VGG → ResNet → Inception → Vision Transformer. Her named turning points: AlexNet, ResNet, Inception, ViT. By 2017 model error had dropped below human error on that benchmark.

CLIP and contrastive learning

The question CLIP answers: can text and image modalities be used together for classification, since a caption explains an image and an image resembles its caption?

Two encodersA text encoder and an image encoder produce embeddings.
Contrastive lossPull matched image–text pairs together; push mismatched pairs apart.
Training goalMaximise the diagonal (positive pairs), minimise everything off it.
InferenceGiven an image, the text whose pair scores highest is the predicted class.

This is what makes zero-shot classification possible: no example of the class needs to be shown to the model, because it has learned an alignment rather than a fixed label set.

Trap she corrected in class: CLIP is not SAM. CLIP is a classification model trained with a contrastive vision–language objective. SAM is a high-end promptable segmentation model trained for a different task. Different task, different training. Do not swap them in an answer.

  • Some domains — medical, food processing — use far more than three channels: 32, 100 or more.

Compression — and the real problem behind it

Why compress: to transmit, to reduce storage, to fit limited computation, and because the hardware at the far end may not be able to process full resolution at all.

Her live example: a police surveillance deployment where every frame from every camera is sent to a cloud server. Two problems. First, cost — surveillance runs continuously, so every frame is billed. Second, existing compression struggles when motion is fast, and missed instants matter enormously to the police. What they want is on-camera intelligence: if nothing is happening, do not send the frame. She offered this as an open real-world problem, which makes it good material for a scenario answer.

How JPEG-style compression decides what to drop: convert the image into frequency space. That separation into high and low frequency is the importance ranking — keep the low-frequency content, aggressively reduce the high-frequency content, and the information loss stays perceptually small.

PNG is lossless; JPG is lossy. A JPG looks smoother because intensity detail has been removed.

Video frame types

I-frame (intra)

Self-contained keyframe, decodable alone. It is the reference the other frames are built from.

P-frame (predicted)

Inferred from the previous frame. If I know where the object was at time t, I can predict where it can reasonably be at t+1 and skip sending the full frame.

B-frame (bidirectional)

Uses frames at t and t+2 to reconstruct t+1. Best compression, strongest requirement.

The assumption under all of it, which she asked the class to state explicitly: consecutive frames are usually very similar.

Her exact worked example of an exam question: “I will not ask you what an I-frame is. I will ask you the limitation of the B-frame.” The answer: a B-frame needs a future frame, so it cannot be used in live streaming, real-time video calls, or anything where t+2 does not exist yet. It also adds latency, memory and decode complexity. A secondary case: very fast action such as sports, where the similarity assumption weakens.

Image restoration as a use case

Two examples she gave. Historically, the Ranger 7 lunar probe imagery. Currently, medical imaging: MRI and X-ray are disturbed by patient movement, breathing and device vibration, so the captured image is distorted. Rather than repeat a 45-minute scan, restore the image with AI. A startup built cheaper, lower-spec MRI hardware, accepted the degraded output, and used image-to-image translation to recover high-end quality — saving 25 to 30 minutes per scan.

A student asked how the model can create detail that was never captured. Her answer is the honest one: it is generalisation, not recovery. The model learns a mapping from low-resolution to high-resolution images during training and then infers for new inputs. It is inference at deployment, not the actual data.

LECTURE 3

Histogram, brightness, contrast, dynamic range, noise

histogram · brightness · contrast · dynamic range · equalisation · log transform · slicing · noise · filters · optical flow

The histogram, and its one limitation

A histogram counts how often each intensity value occurs. For an 8-bit grayscale image that is 256 distinct levels. Plotted together the bars start to look like a distribution, but it remains purely statistical.

The point she built a whole slide around: a histogram tells you how many, never where. There is no pixel-location information in it at all. Consequence, and a very likely exam line: three completely different images can have exactly the same histogram. Same statistics, different image.

Three terms that are constantly confused

Term Definition What it controls in the histogram
Brightness The average intensity of all pixels — a single scalar Where the distribution sits
Contrast How easily objects can be distinguished; variation relative to average luminance How widely it is spread
Dynamic range The number of distinct brightness levels the image actually uses How many levels are occupied

Say that trio back in one sentence and you have answered a large family of questions: brightness locates the distribution, contrast spreads it, dynamic range counts it.

Reading a histogram

  • Narrow central peak → low contrast. Everything is aggregated at one place, so the intensity range is not being used.
  • Wide spread → good contrast.
  • Peaks at both ends → high contrast, but with possible loss of detail. Push contrast too far and the image starts to look like noise.
  • Flat, no underlying pattern → overexposed; smooth, dull, no distinguishing structure.

Histogram equalisation — the worked example she did on the board

A 4×4, 8-bit patch containing only the values 80, 100, 120 and 140, each appearing four times. Range used: 80 to 140 out of a possible 0 to 255. The spread is the problem.

PMF: each value = 4 / 16 = 0.25 CDF: 80 → 0.25 · 100 → 0.50 · 120 → 0.75 · 140 → 1.00 new = (CDF − CDF_min) / (1 − CDF_min) × 255
Original CDF After mapping Appearance
80 0.25 0 Black
100 0.50 85 Dark grey
120 0.75 170 Light grey
140 1.00 255 White

A range of 80–140 has become 0–255. Contrast improved because variation between neighbouring pixels increased. Note her caveat: equalisation does not make the histogram perfectly flat — it makes better use of the available intensity range.

Two other point operations

Image negative

g(x,y) = 255 − f(x,y)

Take the highest possible value and subtract the actual value. This is what your phone is doing behind a filter button.

Log transform

s = c · log(1 + r)

The log penalises large values more than small ones, so it compresses the bright end harder than the dark end and rebalances the distribution.

Why you would compress dynamic range at all — this looks contradictory, since high dynamic range is supposed to be good. Her case: an image where a few pixels are extremely bright while most are dark. The dynamic range is technically wide, but the bright pixels dominate the display range and all the detail in the dark regions becomes unreadable. Log transform pulls the bright end in so most of the image information becomes visible simultaneously. Natural domains: medical imaging and remote sensing / satellite data, where the fine detail hiding in dark regions is the entire point.
Follow-up she asked immediately: what happens if you feed that dark-dominated image straight to the model without pre-processing? The answer she wanted is about learning, not about visibility: the dominant dark region biases the model. It learns the dominant region well and fails to pick out the fine structures you actually care about.

Gray-level slicing

Keep intensities in a chosen range [A, B] bright and push everything else dark, so a specific structure stands out. Two benefits, and she wanted both: it focuses the model on the region that matters, and it reduces computation — which is significant in video, where cost is the binding constraint.

The subtle distinction, which is very examinable. Earlier she said a dark-dominated image harms learning. Here she recommends making regions completely dark. Why is that not a contradiction? Because in the first case the dark region still carries variation, so the model learns that distribution and is dominated by it. In slicing the suppressed region is made completely dark — there is no pattern left to learn, so it cannot dominate. Her medical framing: a radiologist finds a small tumour more easily on a cropped region than on a full high-resolution scan, and so does the model.

Noise

Type Behaviour Where it comes from Filter
Gaussian / white Many small random changes — 116 becomes 118. Invisible to the eye but present. Ambient capture conditions Gaussian or averaging
Impulse / salt-and-pepper A few pixels jump to extremes — 0 or 255 Typically during transmission Median
Speckle Granular and multiplicative; values change gradually rather than jumping Ultrasound, MRI Median / adaptive
Shot / Poisson Photon-counting variation Low-light capture Denoising, variance stabilisation
Salt-and-pepper vs speckle, the answer she gave to a direct question: impulse noise is a sudden jump to an extreme; speckle changes values gradually and by multiplication rather than addition, though it can still end up at the extremes. Salt-and-pepper is the base pattern and can be combined with other noise models — it is not exclusive to impulse noise.
The counter-intuitive point, straight from her own research: noise is not only something you remove. Medical benchmark images are unrealistically clean, so reviewers objected that results did not reflect real conditions. The fix was to inject noise at inference — dataset + X noise, + Y noise, + Z noise, then combinations — to demonstrate robustness. Denoising models go further: they train on noisy data so that robustness is learned. If a question asks why you would add noise deliberately, this is the answer.

Filters

  • Averaging — a 1/9 kernel over a 3×3 neighbourhood. Smooths.
  • Median — replace each point with the median of its neighbours. Removes impulse noise without smearing edges.
  • Sharpening — emphasises edges and fine detail.
  • All of the above operate in the spatial domain (kernels directly on pixels). The alternative is filtering in the frequency domain.

Optical flow, and the first sight of HOF/MBH

Optical flow estimates apparent motion between consecutive frames. Every flow vector carries direction and magnitude, and colour in a flow visualisation encodes direction and speed.

Type Covers Choose it when
Sparse Selected informative points Computation is limited, or the background is uniform and only the object matters — no point re-tracking a static background
Dense Nearly every pixel Crowded scenes, pedestrian detection. Expensive.
Her question to the class: two people walk in opposite directions past a fixed central camera. Same HOF or different? Different — HOF is direction-sensitive, so the velocity vectors land in opposite direction bins. The best student answer added the reason cleanly: the descriptor encodes motion direction, so reversing direction reverses the histogram.
And the follow-up: can HOF and MBH describe appearance and shape? No. There is no colour information in a gradient- or flow-based descriptor at all. And even HOG gives you only a high-level blob structure — enough to say “this is a human” or “this is an animal,” not enough to separate a fox from a dog.
LECTURE 4

HOF and MBH properly, video understanding, and detection opens

HOF · MBH · action pipeline · interpolation as a test · re-identification · inductive bias · sliding window · anchors

She reopened this lecture specifically because the HOF/MBH distinction had not landed the previous day. That signals it is examinable.

The one-line separation: HOF asks which way is motion going? MBH asks where does motion change?

HOF, step by step

1 · Two framesTake consecutive frames t and t+1.
2 · Optical flowEstimate motion vectors for pixels or regions.
3 · Bin by directionLeft, right, up, down, diagonals.
4 · HistogramTallest bin = the dominant motion direction.

Person walking right → the rightward bin dominates. Person jumping → the upward bin dominates. Note the wording she settled on: HOF captures the dominant motion direction.

MBH, and why it exists

MBH is built from gradients of the flow field rather than the flow itself. Uniform motion therefore produces a weak response, and places where motion differs from its surroundings produce a strong one.

Her cycling example, which is the cleanest way to hold the distinction. A person cycles left to right, camera fixed. HOF: rightward. MBH: the upper body is almost static while the legs are pedalling, so the strong response appears around the legs — that is where the motion changes. MBH is drawing attention to a motion discontinuity.
Correction she made in class: the “boundary” in Motion Boundary Histogram is not an object bounding box. It is not an outline drawn around the object. It is a highlighted region where motion differs from nearby regions — the legs light up, the static torso does not.
Aspect HOF MBH
Question Which way is motion happening? Where does motion change?
Captures Motion direction Motion boundaries / motion differences
Good for Clear directional motion, stable camera Camera motion, object–background separation
Weakness Can be dominated by global camera motion Less direct for a pure direction summary
Memory line How things move Where motion differs
The two questions she set, with her answers.
Q1. Person walking while the camera pans right — which descriptor isolates the person's independent motion? MBH, because camera motion makes the entire scene appear to move and HOF then reports rightward motion everywhere, mixing camera, background and person.
Q2. Fixed camera, ball moving upward against a still wall — which captures the upward direction? HOF, because direction is the clue and the background is stable.
A precision point she insisted on. A student said HOF “fails” under camera motion. She corrected the wording: it does not fail — optical flow is genuinely there, the camera really is moving, so the descriptor is reporting something true. The right phrasing is that HOF does not apply well or gets dominated. Use her wording in an answer.

Still relevant today? Yes, on resource-constrained hardware — she named microcontroller-class devices with kilobytes of memory, where a deep model simply cannot be deployed. Otherwise deep models outperform.

Her framing of why this matters: this is what “handcrafted” means. Decide the features yourself, extract them, feed them in. Today we push the raw input into a black box and read the output.

Interpolation — the part most people misread

This is not data augmentation and not image generation. It is an evaluation technique for generative models. Take two real data points — say a dog image and a cat image. Walk the latent space between them in, say, ten steps and generate the intermediate images. If the transformation is seamless, the model has genuinely learned the data distribution. If it jumps or breaks, the model has memorised.

It complements Inception Score and FID, which measure quality and diversity but cannot detect memorisation. Her observation: most papers report only IS and FID, and only strong papers demonstrate robustness this way. The same idea extends to video.

Video understanding

  • Stabilisation — hand-held capture introduces vibration; stabilise before analysis, not just for aesthetics.
  • Denoising — poor camera quality degrades every downstream task.
  • Person re-identification — given one portrait, find that person throughout the footage even as pose, clothing and lighting change. Assign a unique ID on entry. Campus security: one person enters at gate A and exits at gate B, and you need the track.
  • Object re-identification — rather than detecting independently in every frame, share detections over time using motion and scene consistency. This is an efficiency argument as much as an accuracy one.
  • Context — the same activity in two different contexts can be two different classes. Graph neural networks let you carry relationships between people, or between locations, across the video.
  • The modern shape — summarise the video into text, then use text plus video as the input. Now you can ask “why did this accident happen, and who was more at fault?” and retrieve only the relevant chunk instead of scanning everything. Memory networks store what happened when.

Detection opens: what a detector predicts

Detection is a multi-task problem: a classification model and a regression model together. Three outputs per object: class label, bounding box, confidence score.

Inductive bias — her 2, 4, 6, __ example. You answer 8, assuming the rule is “add 2.” But it could be 10, if the rule is “add the previous two.” Every ML model makes an assumption like this in order to predict at all. That assumption is inductive bias, and it is the source of uncertainty in the prediction. The confidence score is our handle on that uncertainty, and the research area is uncertainty quantification. Her point: a model must be accurate and confident — a model that acts on a 10% confidence prediction in a safety-critical deployment is dangerous. Domains she accepted: medical diagnosis, drug discovery, weather and cyclone prediction, fraud detection, finance, surveillance.

Sliding window, and why it breaks

The naive solution: slide a window across the image, classify each position as object or background. She called it the equivalent of doing multiplication by repeated addition — it works, and it does not scale.

LocationWhere should the window be placed?
ScaleObjects may be tiny or very large.
ShapePeople are tall, cars are wide — one aspect ratio cannot serve both.
ComputationMillions of window evaluations.
ContextA tight window loses the scene — you cannot tell the person is at a beach.

And the stride is a trap in both directions: a coarse stride is fast but misses objects; a fine stride is accurate but expensive.

The move that unlocks everything after this: stop trying to place the window perfectly. Place a rough box — an anchor — and then learn the correction. Rough estimate first, refine second.
LECTURE 5

Anchors, box regression, and the loss functions that make detection work

anchors · box encoding · Huber loss · focal loss · hard mining · NMS · IoU · one-stage vs two-stage · RetinaNet · FPN

Anchor boxes and bounding-box regression

An anchor is a predefined rough box. The network predicts four corrections — Δx, Δy, Δw, Δh — and anchor plus correction gives the final box. Because there are four continuous values to produce, this is a regression problem, hence “bounding-box regression.”

Why multiple anchor shapes? A tall anchor suits pedestrians, a wide anchor suits cars and buses, and objects occupy wildly different fractions of the image. Varying scale and aspect ratio at each grid point raises the probability that at least one starting box roughly matches each object.

The framework she wants you to carry into your own thesis. Any model design has three dimensions: architecture, loss function, optimisation. Roughly everyone works on the first two; optimisation needs a heavy mathematics background and attracts very few people. Her two illustrations: DC-GAN was an architectural contribution — add convolution layers and normalisation to GAN. W-GAN was a loss contribution — replace the objective with a distance between the real and predicted distributions, converting the discriminator's job from classification to regression. Both are cited on the order of tens of thousands of times. The lesson: a contribution does not have to be a new architecture.

Softmax vs sigmoid, since it comes up repeatedly

  • Sigmoid — binary. Outputs do not sum to one; 0.9 and 0.5 can coexist.
  • Softmax — multi-class. It enforces competition: the total probability is 1, so if one class takes 0.7, the remaining 0.3 must be divided among the rest.
  • Softmax can be used for binary classification. Sigmoid cannot create competition.

Assigning targets to anchors

  1. Match each anchor to a ground-truth box — by Euclidean distance or overlap area. She accepted Manhattan distance too: the choice depends on the problem.
  2. Give each matched anchor a classification target and a regression target.
  3. Anchors with no matching ground truth are labelled class 0 and get no regression target.

Box encoding — the two design decisions

Normalise the centre offsets by anchor size

Otherwise the learning target is not comparable across small and large objects. Same reason you normalise age against income: without it, the large-valued feature dominates.

min-max: (x − xmin) / (xmax − xmin)

Take log of width and height

Log brings exponentially-spread values onto one scale, and it makes scaling behave symmetrically: enlargement becomes a positive change and shrinking a negative one, of comparable magnitude.

Why not just use MSE? Her reasoning is about training dynamics, not statistics. Detection starts from deliberately rough anchor estimates, so early in training the errors are large. MSE would hand the model an enormous penalty at exactly the moment it knows least, and learning stalls. Heavy penalties are useful once the model is stable, or to escape a local minimum — not at the start. Her analogy: you do not hold the major exam in the third class. You give smooth feedback first — assignments, quizzes — and the big test later. Hence the name: smooth L1. Near zero it behaves quadratically; far from zero it becomes linear.

The classification loss problem: background dominates

Picture a landscape photo. A third of it is sky, and objects occupy a thin band. Anchor after anchor is labelled background. Four responses, and she wanted them distinguished:

Technique What it does Category
Subsample negatives Do not use every background anchor Data / sampling
Down-weight negatives Give background anchors a lower weight rather than an equal one Weighting
Online hard mining Separate hard from easy examples and drop the easy ones Mining — a data process
Focal loss Modifies cross-entropy so well-classified easy examples contribute far less; γ tunes the strength Loss function
She was asked whether focal loss and down-weighting are the same thing. The idea is similar — more weight on hard examples, less on easy ones — but the mechanism is different. One is a loss function; the other is mining, a data process. Do not conflate them in an answer. She connected this to token pruning in LLMs: removing redundant tokens from a prompt to save computation is the same instinct applied to a different resource.

Cross-entropy, for completeness: −[y log p + (1−y) log(1−p)]. Her working definition of entropy — randomness, impurity, disorder. Her test for whether you actually understand it: what is the entropy of a messy desk? High. A tidy one? Low. Abstract it until the definition survives the change of domain.

Non-maximum suppression

Many anchors fire on the same object, each with a slightly different box and score. NMS: keep the highest-scoring box, remove every box overlapping it above an IoU threshold, repeat on what remains.

IoU = area of overlap / area of union  ·  0 = disjoint · 1 = identical

Do not write “one-stage is fast, two-stage is accurate” as a rule. She said this directly, twice. There is no such universal claim — it depends on your domain. Modern systems blur the distinction. Also note her terminology point: a stage may contain multiple steps, but multiple steps do not make multiple stages. Stages can be connected or disconnected.

RetinaNet and the feature pyramid

RetinaNet (2017) = ResNet backbone + FPN multi-resolution features + focal loss + careful initialisation of the classification bias.

The CNN assumption she made the class state: hierarchical processing. Edges first, broader features next, understanding last — and resolution keeps reducing along the way. Every convolutional model shares it.

The problem FPN solves. High-resolution feature maps live at early layers, where semantics are weak. So the maps that could detect small objects cannot take advantage of the network's depth. Simply picking several layers from the backbone does not fix it.

The fix. Go down as usual, then add a top-down path that fuses each deeper map back into the larger one — element-wise summation or another fusion. Information now flows both ways: the large map gains semantics, the small map keeps detail.

The mechanical detail she turned into homework: two feature maps of different sizes cannot be fused directly. You use a 1×1 convolution to bring them into matching dimensions first. Fusion is a second, separate decision. Also note the classification and regression sub-networks share weights across pyramid levels — you do not train a separate head per level.

Faster R-CNN, and how a detector is evaluated

Faster R-CNN in four steps

1 · Extract featuresShared CNN backbone over the whole image.
2 · Propose regionsThe RPN predicts likely object regions — class-agnostic.
3 · RoI featuresEach proposal becomes a fixed-size feature representation.
4 · Classify and refinePredict the class and improve the box.

Class-agnostic means stage one is not identifying classes at all — it is only finding boxes for objects. Same construction as “task-agnostic”: no task or class identity is used to process the information.

Stage 1 asks where might objects be? Stage 2 asks what is each object, and where exactly? That gives four losses in total: RPN classification and localisation, then final classification and localisation.

Multi-stage training — three genuine variants

Variant Mechanism Underlying assumption
Stage-wise Each stage has its own loss, confined to that stage The stages are independent
Joint / end-to-end One objective; backpropagation runs from the last stage to the first The stages are dependent
Hybrid Train stage-wise, then jointly refine — or freeze stage 1 and train stage 2 Mixed
Her analogy for dependence: a student's semester-1 performance is highly informative about semester 2. The stages are not independent, so you optimise them together. Faster R-CNN is trained jointly.

Why RoI resizing uses bilinear interpolation

Proposals arrive at different sizes and must become a fixed grid. A naive resize from 256×256 to 32×32 loses precision — and, more importantly, loses the relative relationships between objects. Bilinear interpolation constructs each new data point from its neighbouring cells, the same instinct as filling a missing value with the mean or median of its neighbours.

The honest trade-off she confirmed when challenged: yes, the result is lower resolution and can look blurry. That is what “low resolution” means. What is preserved is the relative structure between objects — and that is what stage 2 needs.

Datasets, and the long tail

  • Pascal VOC — 20 classes, roughly 5,000 images.
  • MS COCO — 80 classes, 120K images. Effectively the default in serious papers.
Long-tailed dataset problem. Plot boxes per class, sorted. A handful of classes have very many boxes; a long tail of classes have very few. She was explicit that this is not outliers and not plain class imbalance: it is more severe, and the imbalance is across semantic categories rather than merely foreground versus background. Every major detection dataset shows it.

Why evaluating a detector is harder than evaluating a classifier

Classification is easy: 75% accuracy, done. Detection adds two problems:

  1. The metric must handle location error. The predicted box partially overlaps the ground truth. Correct or wrong? There is no answer without a threshold.
  2. Over- and under-prediction. Multiple boxes for one dog; one dog missed entirely.

IoU with a threshold α resolves the first. The threshold is a choice you make, exactly as you choose 0.5 in a classifier.

Outcome Meaning Detection-specific cases
True positive Predicted positive and it is true Box overlaps ground truth above threshold
False positive Predicted positive but it is wrong Overlap below threshold; or one box containing two objects
False negative A real object the model did not find Second dog missed
Her trick for never getting these backwards: read the second word first. “Positive” is what was predicted. “True” or “false” says whether that prediction was correct. True positive = predicted positive, and that is true. False positive = predicted positive, and that is false. She also warned that confusion-matrix layouts differ between books — axes and orderings are a representation choice. Understand it fundamentally; do not memorise a grid.
Precision = TP / (TP + FP) — when the model speaks, how often is it right? Recall = TP / (TP + FN) — of everything I should have found, how much did I find?

They trade off inversely. Push the threshold one way and precision rises while recall falls; push it the other way and the reverse happens. So you sweep the threshold, record the pair at each setting, and plot the precision–recall curve. The area under it summarises the trade-off. AP is computed per class; mAP averages AP across classes, and COCO additionally averages over multiple IoU thresholds.

Which one you want depends on the cost of each error. In fraud detection and shoplifting, missing a real case is the expensive failure, so you favour recall. In applications where a false alarm carries a real cost — an incorrect charge at an automated checkout — you favour precision. Medical is the case worth thinking about rather than memorising: missing an abnormality is usually the costlier error, which argues for recall; but a screening system that flags everything is useless, which argues for precision. State the cost structure, then pick. That is the answer she is looking for.

Practical selection

Pareto optimal curve. Not the 80/20 principle — she corrected a student who reached for that. It is the trade-off between effectiveness and efficiency: to make a model more accurate you add layers and modules, which consumes more resources. You cannot improve one without making the other worse. The job is to locate the point where both are acceptable.
  • Lower input resolution → much faster, and it misses small objects.
  • Fewer proposals → much faster, and mAP drops.
  • Backbone → ResNet and Inception-ResNet give higher mAP; MobileNet is efficient with lower mAP.
  • Transfer learning → initialise from a model pre-trained on another dataset. Larger pre-training is better.
  • There is no rule that a given meta-architecture is right for a given domain. Evaluate, then decide.

Semantic segmentation: why the CNN you have will not do

Segmentation = semantic understanding + spatial precision. Hold those two words; the entire lecture is the tension between them.

Why a bounding box is not enough

A box contains the object and background, and gives approximate location. If an autonomous vehicle must know the exact drivable road pixels, or a clinician must know a tumour's true area and boundary, a rectangle does not answer the question. Applications she listed: road free-space estimation, tumour boundary and area, crop versus weed separation, land-cover mapping, object interaction with the environment.

A student asked the obvious question: if segmentation is better, why not use it everywhere? Two reasons, and she wanted both. (1) Computation — this is dense prediction; a 512×512 image is 262,144 pixels to classify. (2) Annotation — someone must label every pixel, by hand, and in medical work that someone must be an expert. This is precisely why medical data collection is so hard. Her rule: if classification can answer your question, do not reach for segmentation.

Semantic, instance, panoptic

Semantic

One class per pixel. All three people are “person”; all cars share one label. No counting.

Instance

Person 1, person 2, person 3. Adds the question which individual object is it?

Panoptic

Class and instance together — “things” plus “stuff.”

Her overlapping-cars question. Two cars touch. Under semantic segmentation they receive the same label and the same colour, so they merge into one large blob that reads as a single big car. There is no counting and no separation. Instance segmentation separates them. This is exactly why instance segmentation emerged — semantic segmentation was failing on autonomous-driving cases — and why it is used to distinguish two tumour types within the same scan.

The output tensor

Network output: H × W × C logits  →  argmax over C  →  H × W mask

For a 256×256 image with 5 classes the output before argmax is 256×256×5 — one probability map per class — and the final mask is 256×256. The output resolution must match the input, because you are classifying every pixel; shrink it and you have lost information by construction.

Two lines she repeated: the network does not output a coloured picture. It outputs scores and probability maps; we convert those into labels afterwards. And: semantic segmentation is not one classification task — it is classification repeated over the whole spatial grid.

Softmax is used rather than sigmoid because the per-pixel probabilities must sum to one and compete: ezi / Σk ezk. With sigmoid, 0.9 and 0.5 could both appear and nothing forces a decision.

Why a classification CNN cannot be reused directly

Two independent reasons, and a strong answer names both.

1 · Resolution collapse

Convolution and pooling continuously reduce the spatial grid; a fully connected layer or global pooling then produces a single label. A 1×1×1000 ImageNet output has no spatial grid left at all, so it is useless for segmentation.

2 · Invariance

CNNs are built on translation invariance — a dog in the left corner, the right corner or the middle is still classified as a dog. Classification wants that, because exact location is unimportant. Segmentation needs the opposite: every boundary matters, small objects must not disappear, spatial layout must survive.

Receptive field, and the downsampling bargain

The receptive field is the region of the input that a given kernel or neuron effectively sees — its context. As resolution decreases through the network, the receptive field grows: local context becomes global context.

Downsampling Gives you Costs you
Useful because Larger receptive field, stronger semantics — the what —
Harmful because — Precise location, boundaries, thin structures, small objects — the where

Trace a 16×8 patch: 4×2 after downsampling, then 2×1, then a single cell. Every boundary and every thin structure is gone.

The question that sets up the whole second half of the course. Suppose an encoder–decoder has symmetric resolutions, so whatever is downsampled is later upsampled. If the encoder never captured the lane marking, can the decoder perfectly reconstruct it? No. Information discarded in encoding cannot be recovered in decoding. That single fact motivates skip connections.

The architecture chain, with the failure that drives each step

FCN naming, since she worked it live: FCN-32s on a 256×256 input gives 256/32 = 8×8; FCN-16s gives 16×16. The number is the downsampling factor.

The methodological point she wanted more than any architecture name: architecture design is not arbitrary and not hit-and-try. It is driven by failures. If you cannot state what the previous approach failed at, you have not understood why the new one exists — and, she added, you are not ready to propose research of your own. Identifying the problem is half the work.

Upsampling, three ways

Method Mechanism Limitation
Nearest neighbour Copy the nearest value — a 2×2 becomes 4×4 with each element repeated four times Blocky; object shapes and boundaries are lost
Bilinear Blend neighbouring values — 0 and 100 become 0, 25, 50, 75 Smooth but blurs boundaries; no semantic understanding. Fine for resizing, not for segmentation
Transposed convolution Learned upsampling filters — the convolution run in reverse, usually followed by a conv layer for refinement Still cannot reconstruct what the encoder discarded

Why learn the upsampling rather than fix it? Because different classes have different boundary shapes — a road boundary behaves nothing like a person boundary — and a fixed interpolation rule cannot express that.

U-Net

Encoder (contracting path) → bottleneck → decoder (expanding path), with skip connections carrying each encoder feature map across to the matching decoder resolution. Fusion is a concatenation or an element-wise sum, and she was firm that the choice is domain-dependent: concatenation is not automatically correct, and part of your job is knowing when it will not work.

Why U-Net dominates medical imaging. Medical and scientific images need precise boundaries and usually come with limited training data — and U-Net is unusually strong under both constraints. Her figure: roughly 99.9% of the medical segmentation papers she saw two years ago used U-Net, and researchers reached for it even for classification and detection.

Context, dilation, and how you actually train and score a segmenter

What the bottleneck is for

Her answer to “why do we need a bottleneck?”: it highlights the important features. Feed in a face and the bottleneck retains eyes, nose, forehead, cheeks — the distinguishing information — in compressed form. The reconstruction loss between the input X and the output X̂ is what trains it; minimise that and the bottleneck representation improves.

The aside worth keeping. An autoencoder encodes each feature as a fixed point — 1.1 for the nose, 1.3 for something else. A variational autoencoder encodes each feature as a distribution. To generate a face you then sample one point per feature and assemble them, which is why the outputs are diverse. She called this the origin of generative modelling: you stop using a fixed data point and start sampling from a learned distribution.

Boundary problem versus context problem

This is the cleanest diagnostic split in the whole course, and she ended the lecture testing it.

Boundary problem Context problem
Symptom Edges are thin or irregular; the mask leaks outside the object A local patch is genuinely ambiguous — a grey region could be road, wall or roof
Cause Spatial precision lost in downsampling The receptive field is too small to disambiguate
Fix High-resolution features — skip connections, stronger decoder Deeper features and larger receptive field — wider context
But you cannot just enlarge the receptive field. In a standard CNN that raises computational cost significantly, and if it becomes too large you start losing fine spatial precision again. So you need both at once — and, critically, local and global information are highly correlated, so processing them separately loses information. They must be learned together. That is the argument for multi-scale design.

Dilated (atrous) convolution

Instead of a compact 3×3 kernel, insert spacing between the sampled positions. The kernel still has nine weights and still costs the same, but it now covers a wider area.

Rate 1Standard convolution. Local view, fine detail.
Rate 2Medium view. Object-level context.
Rate 4+Wide view. Scene-level cues.
The one sentence to memorise: dilated convolution increases the receptive field without pooling, and pooling is what was losing the information. You gain context while keeping the spatial grid.

It addresses all three problems at once: downsampling loses detail, objects appear at many scales, local patches are ambiguous. The rate r is a hyperparameter — she was asked directly whether an adaptive algorithm could choose it from the input and said no, there is no such solution; you set it.

ASPP and DeepLabv3+

ASPP — Atrous Spatial Pyramid Pooling. The key structural point: standard convolution layers are sequential; here the dilated branches run in parallel at different rates, and their outputs are concatenated or otherwise fused. One branch sees fine local structure, another sees a broad region, and combining them handles different object scales.

DeepLabv3+ = ASPP (context → WHAT) + decoder using low-level features (detail → WHERE)

Which is the “what + where” principle stated one last time, now as an architecture.

Training

ML design has three parts: data, model, training and evaluation. Architecture was the model. This is the rest.

Every image is paired with a pixel-level mask stored as class IDs — 0 = sky, 1 = building, 2 = road, 3 = car, 4 = person.

Ignore labels. Not every pixel is useful: some lie outside the annotated region, some are genuinely uncertain at a boundary, some are too ambiguous, some were never labelled. Those are excluded from the loss and the model is not penalised for them. Her justification is worth quoting in an answer: boundary pixels are hard to annotate perfectly, so ignoring uncertain pixels makes training fair.

Logits are the scores produced before the activation function. Logits → softmax → argmax → pixel labels. Score and logit are interchangeable words.

Class imbalance, and why it is a safety issue

Her concrete numbers: in a street scene, road might cover 50,000 pixels while pedestrian covers 1,000. Train on that and the model becomes excellent at road segmentation and poor at pedestrians. Her conclusion, stated plainly: this is why the question of whether autonomous vehicles are safe for pedestrians was a real one. Two fixes — weighted cross-entropy (raise the weight on rare, safety-critical classes) and focal loss (down-weight easy, well-classified background pixels and focus on hard ones).

Ground truth 100 pixels, prediction 120, overlap 80. Notice that Dice and IoU measure the same overlap and give different numbers. They are not interchangeable.

The pixel-accuracy trap, which is nearly certain to appear. 9,000 background pixels, 1,000 tumour pixels. Predict background everywhere: 9,000/10,000 = 90% accuracy, and tumour Dice and IoU are both 0. The fix is mIoU — compute IoU per class and average — which forces every class to be evaluated so rare classes cannot hide behind dominant ones.
Boundary quality is a separate axis. A mask can have good overlap and still have poor edges. In surgical planning, lane boundaries, vessels and thin structures, a slight area error with sharper boundaries is preferable to good overlap with blurry ones. Overlap metrics will not tell you this — inspect the masks.
Asked whether Dice can be the loss instead of just the metric, her answer was careful: evaluation metrics and loss functions are generally kept separate, but nothing prevents you from designing a loss that penalises missing overlap. You can also combine: α·CrossEntropy + β·Dice + γ·Boundary, weighting each term by how much it matters to your problem.

Data augmentation and two practical traps

  • Apply the same geometric transform to the image and to the mask — flip, crop, scale, rotate. If you rotate the image 90°, rotate the mask 90°.
  • Colour jitter and noise go on the image only.
  • Never resize a mask with bilinear interpolation. Class IDs are not continuous values — blending road = 1 with car = 2 yields 1.5, which is not a class. Use nearest neighbour.
Data leakage, which she called self-deception. Compute normalisation statistics — min, max, or z-score parameters — on the training set only. If you fit them on the whole dataset, the test distribution has leaked into training: the model now knows the test data's range, performs well, and that performance is not real. Keep train, validation and test genuinely separate if you intend to deploy. Her framing: if you only want to show a result you can cut corners; if you want to deploy, you cannot.

Where segmentation has moved

  • Vision Transformer backbones. The difference from a text transformer is simply what a token is: an image is split into patches, and each patch is a token. Self-attention is expensive but captures local evidence and global context simultaneously.
  • Hierarchical transformers — multi-scale by construction, so small and large objects are both served.
  • Mask classification. A genuine reframing: instead of predicting a class independently at every pixel, predict a set of mask and class pairs. The problem moves from dense prediction to classification of masks.
  • SAM (Meta, 2023) — promptable segmentation. Give a point, a box, or text-like input and get a mask back.
  • Language-guided segmentation — “segment the red car,” “find tumour-like regions.”
A distinction she drew when asked, and it is a fair exam question. Language conditioning means text acts as a control signal on the model — “make this face smiling” conditions the generation of an image you already supplied. Language guided means the text is used as an input alongside the image, the way CLIP consumes vision and language together. Condition versus input.

Scenario drill bank

Forty-eight questions in her format. Read the situation, commit to an answer out loud, then open it. Questions marked asked were put to the class directly.

Representation, processing and noise

Three images produce identical histograms. Are they the same image?asked

No. A histogram records only how often each intensity occurs — it carries no location information whatsoever. Identical statistics are entirely compatible with completely different spatial arrangements.

An image looks dull and washed out. Which of brightness, contrast or dynamic range is the problem, and how would you confirm it?

Most likely contrast, confirmed by a narrow, crowded histogram. Contrast governs the spread. Check brightness separately by looking at where the distribution sits, and dynamic range by counting how many distinct levels are actually occupied — a dull image typically uses few.

You push contrast enhancement hard and the result starts to look like noise. What has happened?

The histogram has developed peaks at both extremes. That is technically high contrast, but detail has been lost in the compression toward the ends. High contrast is not monotonically good.

An astronomy image has a few extremely bright pixels and mostly dark content. Dynamic range is wide. Why is that still a problem, and what do you apply?asked

The bright pixels dominate the available display range, so detail in the dark regions becomes unreadable. Apply a log transform, s = c·log(1+r): the log penalises large values more than small ones, compressing the bright end harder and letting most of the image information be visible at once. Same reasoning for medical and satellite imagery.

Why is feeding a very dark, bright-dominated image straight to a model a problem — and why is deliberately blacking out a region not the same problem?asked

In the first case the dark region still contains variation, so the model learns that distribution and becomes biased toward the dominant region, missing the fine structures you care about. In gray-level slicing the suppressed region is made completely dark — there is no pattern left to learn, so it cannot dominate. Variation is what gets learned, not darkness.

Ultrasound images arrive with granular, gradually-varying corruption. Name the noise and the filter.

Speckle noise — granular and multiplicative, characteristic of ultrasound and MRI. Median or adaptive filtering. Distinguish it from impulse noise, which jumps suddenly to 0 or 255 and usually arises in transmission.

A reviewer says your medical results do not reflect real conditions. Your benchmark images are pristine. What do you do?

Inject noise at inference to demonstrate robustness: evaluate on dataset + X noise, + Y noise, + Z noise, then on combinations. This is her own experimental practice. It is the case where noise is added rather than removed.

You have both PNG and JPG versions of a dataset. Which do you keep for a fine-detail medical task, and why?

PNG — lossless. JPG is lossy and looks smoother precisely because intensity detail has been discarded, and fine detail is the whole point in a medical task.

What is the assumption underlying all video compression, and where does it break?asked

Consecutive frames are usually very similar. It weakens under fast action — sports being the standard example — where consecutive frames differ substantially and prediction from neighbours becomes unreliable.

Give the limitation of the B-frame.asked

A B-frame is reconstructed from both a past and a future frame. In live streaming, real-time video calls or any real-time pipeline, t+2 does not exist yet, so the scheme cannot be used. It also adds latency, memory and decode complexity. This is her own worked example of the kind of question she asks instead of “define I-frame.”

Surveillance cameras stream every frame to a cloud server and the bill is enormous. Propose a direction.

Move intelligence to the camera. If nothing significant is happening, do not transmit, or transmit a minimal representation. This requires local processing that the deployment currently lacks. She presented this as an unsolved real-world problem, so a good answer names the constraint — continuous operation means every frame is billed — as well as the idea.

A cheaper MRI produces degraded scans, and AI restores them. A student objects that the model is inventing detail. Is the objection right?

Partly, and the honest framing is hers: this is generalisation, not recovery. The model learned a mapping from low- to high-resolution images during training and infers at deployment. It is not the actual data. It is defensible when validated properly — held-out sets, medical benchmarks, and radiologist feedback in the loop.

Motion

Person walking while the camera pans right. Which descriptor isolates the person's own motion?asked

MBH. Camera motion makes the entire scene appear to move, so HOF reports rightward motion everywhere and mixes camera, background and person. MBH works on the gradient of the flow field, so uniform motion is suppressed and the person's motion boundaries stand out.

Fixed camera, ball moving upward against a still wall. HOF or MBH?asked

HOF. Direction is the clue and the background is stable, so the upward bin dominates cleanly.

Two people walk past a fixed central camera in opposite directions. Same HOF or different?asked

Different. HOF is direction-sensitive: the velocity vectors land in opposite direction bins, so the histograms are effectively mirrored.

Someone cycles left to right past a fixed camera. Describe both descriptors.asked

HOF: rightward — that is the dominant direction. MBH: a strong response around the legs, because the upper body is nearly static while the legs pedal. MBH is locating where motion changes, not outlining the cyclist.

Is it correct to say HOF "fails" under camera motion?asked

No, and she corrected this wording explicitly. Optical flow is genuinely present — the camera really is moving — so the descriptor is reporting something true. Say it does not apply well, or that it gets dominated by global motion.

Can HOF or MBH tell you an object's colour or exact shape?asked

No. There is no colour information in a gradient- or flow-based descriptor at all. Even HOG gives only a high-level blob structure — enough to say "human" or "animal," not enough to separate a fox from a dog.

Deep models learn motion features automatically. Is MBH obsolete?asked

No, on genuinely constrained hardware — microcontroller-class devices with kilobytes of memory, where a deep model cannot be deployed at all. Elsewhere deep models outperform. Same argument she gave for HOG in Lecture 1.

What does video interpolation evaluate, and why is it not covered by FID?

It tests whether a generative model has learned the distribution or memorised the training set. Take two real data points, generate roughly ten intermediate samples, and check whether the transformation is seamless. Inception Score and FID measure quality and diversity — neither detects memorisation, which is why only stronger papers report interpolation.

A campus needs to know that the person who entered at gate A is the one who left at gate B. Name the task.

Person re-identification: assign a unique ID on entry and match that identity across the footage, tolerating changes in pose, clothing and lighting. Object re-identification is the same idea for objects, and it is also an efficiency mechanism — share detections across time using motion and scene consistency instead of detecting independently in every frame.

Detection

What is inductive bias, and what does it have to do with the confidence score?asked

The assumption a model must make in order to predict at all — her 2, 4, 6, __ example, where 8 assumes "add 2" but 10 would follow "add the previous two." That assumption is the source of prediction uncertainty. The confidence score is our handle on it, and the field is uncertainty quantification. Her point: a model must be accurate and appropriately confident. Acting on a 10% confidence prediction in a safety-critical deployment is the failure case.

List four problems with sliding-window detection.asked

Location (where to place the window), scale (objects tiny to huge), shape (people tall, cars wide — one aspect ratio cannot serve both), and computation (millions of evaluations). A fifth she drew out of the class: a tight window destroys context, so you cannot tell that the person is standing at a beach.

Why does a finer sliding-window stride not simply solve the problem?

The stride is a trap in both directions. Coarse is fast and misses objects; fine is accurate and computationally infeasible. Neither end is acceptable, which is what motivates abandoning exhaustive search for anchors plus learned correction.

Why is an anchor allowed to be a bad box?

Because it is only a starting guess. The network predicts Δx, Δy, Δw, Δh and refines it. Rough estimate first, correction second — that decomposition is what makes detection tractable.

Why encode box size as log(w) and log(h) rather than w and h?asked

Log brings widely-spread values onto one scale, and it makes scaling behave symmetrically: enlargement becomes a positive change and shrinking a negative one, of comparable magnitude. Without it, doubling and halving produce targets of very different sizes.

Why are centre offsets normalised by anchor size?asked

So the learning target is comparable across small and large objects. Same principle as normalising age against income: without it the large-valued quantity dominates learning.

Why is MSE the wrong regression loss for bounding boxes?asked

The reason is about training dynamics, not statistics. Detection starts from deliberately rough anchors, so early errors are large — with an error of 10, MSE gives 100 while Huber gives 9.5, summed over many boxes. That hands the model an enormous penalty exactly when it knows least, and learning stalls. Large penalties are useful once the model is stable or to escape a local minimum, not at the start. Hence smooth L1: quadratic near zero, linear far from it.

A landscape image yields mostly background anchors. Name four responses and classify each.asked

Subsample negatives (sampling), down-weight negatives (weighting), online hard negative mining (a data process), and focal loss (a loss function). She was explicit that focal loss and hard mining share an intent but differ in mechanism — do not treat them as the same thing.

Define entropy without using the word "entropy."asked

Randomness, impurity, disorder. Her test: a messy desk has high entropy; a tidy one has low entropy. She wanted an abstraction that survives moving between domains, and she rejected borrowed technical phrasing that did not.

Three anchors fire on the same pedestrian with scores 0.95, 0.89 and 0.75. What happens?

Non-maximum suppression. Keep the 0.95 box, remove any box whose IoU with it exceeds the threshold, then repeat on what remains. A box at IoU 0.10 with the kept box survives — it is presumably a different object.

Is one-stage detection faster and two-stage more accurate?asked

Do not state it as a rule — she said so twice. One-stage is generally faster and two-stage generally more refined, but there is no universal claim; it depends on the domain, and modern systems blur the distinction. Also: a stage may contain several steps, but several steps do not make several stages.

What exactly does FPN fix?asked

High-resolution feature maps live at early layers where semantics are weak, so the maps capable of detecting small objects cannot benefit from network depth. FPN adds a top-down path that fuses deeper maps back into larger ones, so the large map gains semantics and the small map keeps detail. A 1×1 convolution first matches channel dimensions, since maps of different sizes cannot be fused directly.

Do early CNN layers carry high or low semantic information?asked

Low. Meaning develops with depth. Her textile analogy: the actual quality of the cloth — rough or fine — only becomes clear at the later stages. Her traffic analogy: how local areas connect to distant ones becomes clear later. A fine-grained small object cannot be identified at an early layer because the understanding is not there yet.

Why does RPN produce class-agnostic proposals?

Because stage one's only job is to find where objects might be. No class identity is used or produced. Stage two then asks what each object is and refines the box. Same construction as "task-agnostic": no task identity used in processing.

Why are Faster R-CNN's two stages trained jointly rather than separately?asked

Because the stages are dependent — her analogy is that a student's semester-1 performance strongly predicts semester 2. Stage-wise training assumes independence and confines each loss to its own stage; joint training backpropagates end to end. Hybrid schemes exist: train stage-wise, then refine jointly, or freeze stage 1 and train stage 2.

Why not just call resize() on each region proposal?asked

A naive resize loses precision and, more importantly, the relative relationships between objects. Bilinear interpolation constructs each new point from its neighbours — the same instinct as imputing a missing value from neighbouring values. The output is genuinely lower resolution and can look blurry; what survives is the relative structure, which is what stage two needs.

What is a long-tailed dataset, and why is calling it "class imbalance" insufficient?asked

A few classes have very many annotated boxes and a long tail of classes have very few. She was explicit that this is more severe than plain imbalance and that the imbalance is across semantic categories rather than merely foreground versus background. It is not an outlier problem — outliers are one or two points; this is a structural property of every major detection dataset.

A prediction box partially overlaps the ground truth. Correct or wrong?asked

Unanswerable without a threshold — which is exactly the point. Compute IoU and compare against a chosen α, the same way you choose 0.5 in a classifier. IoU is 0 for disjoint boxes and 1 for identical ones.

One predicted box contains two separate dogs. What is it?

A false positive. Two distinct real objects covered by one box does not count as correct detection of either — and the second dog is simultaneously a false negative.

A shoplifting detection system: precision or recall?asked

Recall. Missing a real case is the expensive failure. Contrast with a system where a false alarm carries real cost — an automated checkout charging a customer incorrectly — where precision dominates. The answer is always derived from the cost structure of each error type, not from habit.

What does the Pareto optimal curve describe here, and what is it not?asked

The trade-off between effectiveness and efficiency: adding layers and modules raises accuracy and consumes more resources, so you cannot improve one without worsening the other. The task is finding the point where both are acceptable. It is not the 80/20 Pareto principle — she corrected a student who offered that.

You halve the input resolution and the model gets much faster. What did you lose?

Small objects, primarily. Fine-grained information is missed. The same shape of trade-off applies to reducing the number of proposals — faster, with mAP dropping — and to choosing MobileNet over ResNet.

Segmentation

An autonomous vehicle must know the exact drivable road surface. Is a bounding box enough?asked

No. A box contains object plus background and gives only approximate location. Free-space estimation needs the actual region shape, so it needs segmentation. The general rule: segmentation when exact shape matters, box when presence and rough location suffice.

If segmentation is strictly more informative, why not use it for everything?asked

Computation — a 512×512 image is 262,144 pixels to classify densely — and annotation, since every pixel must be labelled by hand, by an expert in medical work. This is precisely why medical data collection is so hard. Her rule: if classification can answer the question, do not reach for segmentation.

Two cars overlap. Under semantic segmentation, how many cars are in the mask?asked

Effectively one. They receive the same label and the same colour, so they merge into a single blob that reads as one large car. Semantic segmentation does no counting. Instance segmentation separates them — which is exactly why it emerged, after semantic segmentation kept failing on autonomous-driving cases.

A 128×128 image with 4 classes. What is the output tensor before argmax, and after?asked

Before: 128 × 128 × 4 — one probability map per class. After argmax: 128 × 128. She pressed the class on this until the per-class maps were named.

Why is a 1×1×1000 ImageNet classifier output useless for segmentation?asked

There is no spatial grid left. Segmentation requires a class decision at every spatial location, and that output has collapsed all locations into a single vector.

Give two independent reasons a classification CNN cannot be used directly for segmentation.asked

(1) Resolution collapses through convolution and pooling, so spatial information is destroyed by construction. (2) CNNs are built on translation invariance — a dog anywhere is a dog — which classification actively wants and segmentation cannot tolerate, since every boundary matters and small objects must not disappear.

Downsampling is both useful and harmful. Explain both halves.asked

Useful: it enlarges the receptive field and builds semantics — the what. Harmful: it destroys precise location, boundaries, thin structures and small objects — the where. Segmentation needs both, which is why the rest of the course is architectural attempts to hold them simultaneously.

The encoder never captured the lane markings. Can a symmetric decoder reconstruct them?asked

No. Information discarded in encoding cannot be recovered in decoding, regardless of how good the decoder is. This single fact is the entire motivation for skip connections.

Which upsampling method would you use, and why is the answer not "the smoothest one"?

Transposed convolution — learned upsampling — because different classes have different boundary shapes and a fixed interpolation rule cannot express that. Nearest neighbour is blocky; bilinear is smoother but blurs boundaries and adds no semantic understanding, which makes it fine for resizing and wrong for segmentation. And note the limit: even learned upsampling cannot recover what the encoder discarded.

What information does a U-Net skip connection carry to the decoder?asked

Spatial detail — edges, local texture, precise boundary position. The decoder already has meaning, because the deep features arriving from the bottleneck carry the semantics. What it lacks is where exactly, and that is what the early layers supply.

Why does U-Net dominate medical imaging specifically?asked

Medical and scientific images need precise boundaries and typically come with limited training data, and U-Net is unusually strong under both constraints simultaneously. Her figure: roughly 99.9% of the medical segmentation papers she saw two years ago used it, and people reached for it even for classification and detection.

Concatenation or element-wise sum for the skip fusion?asked

It depends on the domain — and she refused to endorse concatenation as a default. Her framing: fusion choice is also driven by evidence, and part of your job is knowing when concatenation will not work and why.

A mask keeps confusing road with sidewalk. Boundary problem or context problem?asked

Context. The local patch is genuinely ambiguous — two surfaces of similar texture — so the fix is a wider receptive field and broader scene context, i.e. dilated convolution or a DeepLab-style module. A boundary problem looks different: correct class, wrong shape, mask leaking past the edge.

Why can you not just enlarge the CNN receptive field until context is sufficient?asked

Computational cost rises significantly, and if the field becomes too large you start losing fine spatial precision again. You need both scales — and because local and global information are highly correlated, processing them separately loses information. They must be learned together. That is the argument for multi-scale design.

What is the single advantage of dilated convolution over pooling?asked

It increases the receptive field without pooling — and pooling is what was destroying the information. Same kernel, same cost, wider coverage, spatial grid intact.

What is structurally unusual about ASPP compared with normal conv layers?asked

Standard convolution layers are sequential; ASPP runs dilated branches at different rates in parallel and then fuses their outputs. One branch sees fine local structure, another sees a broad region, and the combination handles several object scales at once.

Can the dilation rate be chosen automatically from the input?asked

No — she was asked directly and said there is no adaptive solution that decides it from the problem. r is a hyperparameter you set. The adjacent research area is AutoML and neural architecture search, which searches over combinations — and which she noted itself requires considerable expertise, not a prompt.

Some pixels near the object boundary are genuinely ambiguous. What do you do with them?

Give them an ignore label and exclude them from the loss; the model is not penalised for them. Her justification: boundary pixels cannot be annotated perfectly, so ignoring uncertain pixels makes training fair. The same applies to pixels outside the annotated region and to unlabelled pixels.

Road: 50,000 pixels. Pedestrian: 1,000. What happens, and what do you do?asked

The model becomes excellent at road and poor at pedestrians — her stated reason why pedestrian safety in autonomous vehicles was a genuine open question. Fixes: weighted cross-entropy, raising the weight on rare safety-critical classes, and focal loss, which down-weights easy well-classified background pixels and concentrates on hard ones.

9,000 background pixels, 1,000 tumour pixels. A model predicts background everywhere. Report its accuracy, Dice and IoU.asked

Pixel accuracy 90%. Tumour Dice 0. Tumour IoU 0. This is the canonical demonstration that pixel accuracy is misleading, and the reason to report mIoU — per-class IoU averaged — so that rare classes cannot hide behind dominant ones.

Ground truth 100 pixels, prediction 120, overlap 80. Compute Dice and IoU and say what the difference proves.asked

Dice = 2×80/(120+100) = 0.727. IoU = 80/(100+120−80) = 0.571. Same overlap, different numbers — which is precisely why they are not interchangeable. Convention: Dice in medical, IoU in road and scene segmentation.

Model A has better Dice; model B has visibly sharper edges. Which do you ship for surgical planning?

B. Overlap metrics do not capture boundary quality, and in surgical planning, lane boundaries, vessels and thin structures a slight area error with sharp boundaries beats good overlap with blurry ones. Her instruction: inspect the masks, do not report a single number.

Why not train directly on Dice, since it is what you are measuring?asked

You can — but evaluation metrics and loss functions are generally kept separate, and her answer was careful rather than absolute. Nothing prevents designing a loss that penalises missing overlap, and combined objectives are common: α·CrossEntropy + β·Dice + γ·Boundary, weighted by what matters in your problem.

You rotate every training image by 15°. What else must happen, and what must not?

The mask must be rotated identically — every geometric transform applies to both. Colour jitter and noise apply to the image only. And when resizing the mask, use nearest neighbour: bilinear blending of road = 1 and car = 2 gives 1.5, which is not a class.

Your model scores beautifully on the test set and collapses in deployment. You computed min–max normalisation over the full dataset. Explain.

Data leakage — her term was self-deception. Fitting normalisation statistics on all the data means the test distribution's range was available during training, so the model's apparent generalisation is partly memorised. Compute statistics on the training set only, and keep train, validation and test genuinely separate if you intend to deploy.

A segmenter trained on clear daytime footage degrades badly at night and in rain. Name the failure.asked

Domain shift. Same family as deploying at a new hospital or with a new sensor: the training and deployment distributions differ. Distinguish it from a boundary problem or class confusion, which are failures within the training distribution.

Brain tumour segmentation where missing the tumour is serious. Which metric?asked

Dice — the medical convention, and appropriate because the foreground region is small, which is exactly the case where pixel accuracy misleads and overlap-focused metrics are informative.

What is a token in a Vision Transformer?asked

A patch. The image is divided into patches, and each patch plays the role a word token plays in a text transformer. Self-attention is computationally expensive but captures local evidence and global context simultaneously — which is precisely the tension the whole segmentation half of the course has been about.

How does mask classification differ from dense prediction?

Dense prediction assigns a class independently at every pixel. Mask classification predicts a set of mask and class pairs instead — a genuine reframing of the problem, not just a better backbone. MaskFormer and Mask2Former are the reference points.

Distinguish language-conditioned from language-guided segmentation.asked

Conditioning: text acts as a control signal on the model — "make this face smiling" conditions the generation of an image you supplied. Guided: text is used as an input alongside the image, the way CLIP consumes both together. Condition versus input.

The seven questions she left open

She explicitly declined to answer these in class and assigned them. Unanswered homework from a lecturer who writes her own paper is the highest-yield revision on this page.

1 · Why do we perform histogram equalisation — beyond "it improves contrast"?

She gave the contrast answer herself, then said it was insufficient and told the class to come back with better points. Directions worth preparing: normalising images that arrive from different sources and different exposures onto a common intensity distribution; making full use of the available 8-bit range so downstream features are computed over a usable spread; reducing the model's sensitivity to acquisition conditions; and making low-contrast structures separable before any feature extraction happens at all. She wanted an abstraction, not a restatement.

2 · Name four situations — high brightness, low brightness, high contrast, low contrast

Explicitly set as homework for both cohorts. The framing she wanted: we do not always want high brightness and high contrast. Work out when each is genuinely preferable, and bring a domain for each.

3 · Work out all four camera/subject motion combinations for HOF and MBH

She set up the pair explicitly and handed it back: camera fixed and person moving; person fixed and camera moving; both moving in the same direction; both moving in opposite directions. Reason each one through rather than memorising a table — she declined to give the answers precisely so you would derive them.

4 · Experiment with CNN layer arithmetic and 1×1 convolution

Assigned directly after the FPN fusion discussion. If a feature map is 80×80×128 and you apply a 1×1 convolution, what is the output, and what exactly did the operation change? Then vary the layer configuration and observe. She wanted this done by hand, not looked up.

5 · The A / B / C feature identification, and why the decoder needs two of them

She read out the four-part question and then refused to give answers, calling it easy and leaving it as homework. The features: A — sharp edges, local texture, accurate position. B — object meaning, scene context, coarse layout. C — an upsampled semantic map needing boundary refinement. Which comes from an early encoder layer? Which from the bottleneck? Which is the skip-connection contribution? And why does the decoder need both A and B? Answer all four and you have the whole encoder–decoder argument.

6 · When is Dice loss more appropriate than cross-entropy — and when do you need both?

Asked in the final lecture and returned as an open-ended comparison. Her instruction: compare them at the fundamental level and work out the conditions. She added a specific requirement — do not only say which is better; say in what conditions you need both, since they are independent and can be combined with weights.

7 · Can I-, P- and B-frames be combined, and in which scenarios?

A student asked; she called it a good question and moved on without answering. Worth preparing alongside the B-frame limitation, since real codecs do exactly this and the interesting part is which scenario forces which mix.
